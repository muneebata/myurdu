Tehzeeb · The Art of Aadab

takalluf, titles, and the manners built into the language

Urdu doesn't just have vocabulary, it has manners baked into the grammar. Which 'you' you choose, how many times you refuse food, and what you call the barber are all part of tehzeeb: the courtesy culture the language grew up in. Heritage speakers often know the words but not these rules. This unit is the missing manual.

The phrases

آداب عرض ہےādāb arz hai: I offer my respectsthe full formal version, plain 'ādāb' works too
جنابjanāb: sir / your honorthe safest formal address for a stranger
بزرگbuzurg: an elderelders get greeted first, always
تہذیبtehzīb: culture, refinementthis unit's own name: the whole code of conduct in one word
احترامehtirām: respectehtirām se, respectfully, is how you address an elder
میزبانmezbān: hostthe other half of mehmān-nawāzī
لحاظlihāz: deference, considerationthe restraint you show for someone else's feelings
سلیقہsalīqa: a graceful way of doing thingsuntranslatable praise: the knack of doing something properly
عزتizzat: honour, respectizzat karnā is to honour; izzat is also what a family guards

Good to know

✨ Āp: respect. For elders, strangers, teachers, in-laws… and in many families, even for parents. When in doubt, āp. Nobody was ever offended by too much āp.
✨ Tum: familiar. Friends, younger relatives, close colleagues. Warm, casual, everyday.
✨ Tū: intimate, and dangerous. Used for one's closest childhood friends, small children, and for God in prayer and poetry, where the intimacy is the whole point. Used on a stranger, it's an insult. Treat tū like a loaded word until you've lived in Urdu for years.
✨ Sāhib (from Arabic, 'companion') attaches to nearly any name or role as respect: Ḍākṭar sāhib, Khan sāhib. The feminine counterpart in everyday speech is sāhiba.
✨ Ustād means master, of anything. Your music teacher is ustād, and so is the master mechanic and the master barber. Craft is craft; the title honors skill itself. (Yes, this app's name plays on it.)
✨ Bhāī (brother) and bājī (elder sister) turn strangers into family, the shopkeeper, the rickshaw driver, the neighbor. Urdu extends kinship to the whole street.
