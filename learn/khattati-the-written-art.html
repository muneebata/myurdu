Khattati · The Written Art

from master calligraphers to truck art. Urdu as a thing of beauty

Urdu isn't just spoken and read, it's drawn. The script you've been tracing in Sound School has been an art form for six centuries, practiced by court calligraphers, newspaper kātibs, modern masters, and the anonymous geniuses who paint trucks. This unit is the story of Urdu as a visual art.

Good to know

✨ Nastaliq arose in 14th-century Persia, its very name is a fusion of two older scripts, naskh and ta'līq. Tradition credits the calligrapher Mir Ali Tabrizi with perfecting it.
✨ Unlike flat-line naskh, Nastaliq hangs: words cascade diagonally, letters swell and taper, and the whole line breathes. That beauty is exactly what made it brutally hard to digitize.
✨ Urdu embraced Nastaliq as its own, and Pakistan's preferred style, the Lahori nastaliq, is the one this site renders in.
✨ Well into the 1980s, Urdu newspapers could not be typeset in Nastaliq, so every page of dailies like Jang was hand-written by professional kātibs, then photographed and printed. Every single day.
✨ The revolution came in 1981, when calligrapher-entrepreneur Mirza Ahmad Jamil created Noori Nastaliq with Monotype, the first practical digital Nastaliq font, built from thousands of hand-drawn letter combinations.
✨ The struggle isn't over: much of the internet still renders Urdu in Arabic-style naskh, and keeping true Nastaliq alive digitally remains an active fight, one this site has picked a side in.
