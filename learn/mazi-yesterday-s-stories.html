Mazi · Yesterday's Stories

The past tense — kal finally picks a direction

Since Level 8 you've known that kal means yesterday AND tomorrow. Today the mystery resolves: the verb decides. Learn a handful of past forms and suddenly you can tell stories — which is most of what conversation is.

The phrases

تھاthā — was (male speaker)Maiṉ wahāṉ thā — I was there.
تھیthī — was (female speaker)Same sentence, thī for women — past agrees with gender.
تھےthe — were (plural / respectful)Āp always gets the — respect is plural in Urdu.
کل میں بازار گیاkal maiṉ bāzār gayā — Yesterday I went to the bazaar (m)THE kal payoff: gayā is past, so kal must mean yesterday.
کل میں بازار گئیkal maiṉ bāzār gaī — Yesterday I went to the bazaar (f)gayā → gaī. Your verbs introduce you.
وہ آیاwoh āyā — he cameAnd woh āī — she came.
میں نے کھانا کھایاmaiṉ ne khānā khāyā — I ate foodMeet 'ne' — the little past-tense marker. Learn the phrase whole for now.
میں نے فلم دیکھیmaiṉ ne film dekhī — I watched a filmAnother ne chunk — notice dekhī agrees with film (f).
بہت مزہ آیاbahut mazā āyā — It was great funLiterally 'much fun came'. The standard verdict on any good evening.
آپ کہاں تھے؟āp kahāṉ the? — Where were you?L6's kahāṉ + today's the. The classic aunty opener.

Good to know

✨ The kal mystery, solved: “kal maiṉ gayā” (past verb → yesterday) vs “kal maiṉ jāūṉgā” (future verb → tomorrow). Urdu trusts the verb to carry the calendar.
✨ Past-tense verbs agree with gender — gayā (m) vs gaī (f) — so a single sentence quietly introduces its speaker. Novelists love this; language learners get used to it fast.
✨ That little ne (میں نے) is Urdu's famous ergative marker — it appears with 'doing' verbs in the past. Every textbook devotes a scary chapter to it; every learner masters it by just using phrases like maiṉ ne khāyā until it feels natural. Do that.
