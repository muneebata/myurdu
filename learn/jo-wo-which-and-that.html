Jo–Wo · Which and That

Relative clauses, the hinge of every complex sentence

Urdu doesn't say 'the person who came'. It says 'WHICH person came, THAT person…' — jo opens, wo answers. Master this one echo and long sentences stop being long: they become two short ones holding hands. Half the proverbs you've met already run on it.

The phrases

جو لوگ محنت کرتے ہیں، وہ آگے بڑھتے ہیں۔jo log mehnat karte haiṉ, wo āge baṛhte haiṉ: People who work hard get ahead.Log = people, mehnat = hard work. Jo opens the clause, wo picks it up.
جو آپ کہیں۔jo āp kaheṉ: Whatever you say.The gracious surrender. Compare L22's jab āp kaheṉ — when vs whatever.
جو ہوا، سو ہوا۔jo huā, so huā: What happened, happened.So is wo's older sibling, kept alive in fixed sayings.
یہ وہ کتاب ہے جو میں نے پڑھی۔yeh wo kitāb hai jo maiṉ ne paṛhī: This is the book I read.Paṛhī agrees with kitāb, a she — the L14 ne-rule inside a relative clause.
جو سستا ہے، وہ لے لیں۔jo sastā hai, wo le leṉ: Take whichever is cheap.Bazaar grammar. Sastā from your bargaining days.
جس سے پوچھیں، وہی بتائے گا۔jis se pūchheṉ, wohī batāe gā: Whoever you ask will tell you.Jo bends to jis before se (like kaun → kis, Qawāid Q13). Pūchhnā = to ask.
جہاں چائے، وہاں بات۔jahāṉ chāy, wahāṉ bāt: Where there's chai, there's conversation.Jahāṉ–wahāṉ, the same echo for places.
جب تک آپ ہیں، سب ٹھیک ہے۔jab tak āp haiṉ, sab ṭhīk hai: As long as you're here, all is well.Jab tak = as long as. The echo works for time too.
جو بھی ہو۔jo bhī ho: Whatever happens.The shrug that accepts anything. Bhī widens jo to 'any at all'.
وہی تو!wohī to!: Exactly! That's just it!Wohī = that very thing; the to lands the emphasis. You'll hear this daily.
جس کو دیکھو، مصروف ہے۔jis ko dekho, masrūf hai: Everyone you look at is busy.The city sigh. Masrūf from your phone-call scene.
جو ملے، اس پر شکر۔jo mile, us par shukr: Be thankful for whatever you get.Mile is subjunctive — a maybe-getting. More of that in L27.
جو جیتا، وہی سکندر۔jo jītā, wohī sikandar: Whoever wins is the champion.A famous saying. Sikandar = Alexander the Great, whose name became Urdu's word for a winner.
جہاں چاہ، وہاں راہ۔jahāṉ chāh, wahāṉ rāh: Where there's a will, there's a way.Chāh = wanting (chāhnā's noun); rāh = road. A classic built on L25's own pattern.
جس کا کام، اسی کو ساجے۔jis kā kām, usī ko sāje: Every job suits its own master.A kahāwat: leave the stitching to the darzi. Sāje = befits, an old subjunctive.
جو بوؤ گے، وہ کاٹو گے۔jo bo'o ge, wo kāṭo ge: You will reap what you sow.Bonā = to sow; kāṭnā you know from the kitchen. The proverb every language owns.

Good to know

✨ Urdu builds relative clauses as PAIRS: jo…wo, jahāṉ…wahāṉ, jab…tab, jitnā…utnā, jaisā…waisā. Learn the echo once and every pair works the same way.
✨ Sikandar is Alexander the Great — his name marched into Urdu as the word for an unbeatable winner, which is why jo jītā wohī sikandar needs no translation at a cricket match.
✨ Flip through the Kahawatain unit and count: a good half of the proverbs run on jo–wo. You could already read them; now you can see their skeleton.
